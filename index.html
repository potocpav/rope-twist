    Why Ropes Twist

    Climbing ropes sometimes twist and tangle, but the precise mechanism of how and when is not well understood in the climbing community. Which belay devices, anchor configurations, or coiling methods cause the twist? Does it depend on friction, climber weight or lowering speed? What even is rope twist? This article explores the physics of rope twisting to answer these questions.

    Pavel Potoček · October 2026

    1 Theory

    Rope twist is not an invisible or mysterious property, nor is it caused by the rope sheath moving relative to the rope core (at least not in most cases). Rope twist is simply the rotation of a rope around its own axis. When one end of the rope is held stationary, and the other end is spun, the rope accumulates twist. When the number of twists is high enough, the rope starts buckling and eventually forms loops and becomes tangled.

    Because climbing rope sheath is diagonally braided, ropes bend easily, but are very resistant to torsion - a technical term for twisting along a rope's length (see Figure 6). Given this property, when running around obstacles, ropes will bend, but will remain mostly untwisted. It turns out, rather surprisingly, that this tendency to remain untwisted when following curved paths is what makes the rope spin, and as a result, become twisted and tangled.

    In order to quantify this effect, we need to understand the details of how ropes behave when running around obstacles. We can model rope as a curve running through space. When the curve is planar, the rope bends in an obvious fashion, and no twist is induced. This is the case for a straight rope, but also rope running over a pulley, through a tube-style device, or through some simple anchor configurations.

    1.1 Simple case

    The simplest case which does induce twist is a rope wrapped around a bar at an angle (Figure 3). Running the rope along such a path forces the rope to spin about its own axis at the rate of ω radians per second:

    Mathematical derivation

    The mathematical model comes from Kirchhoff's rod theory. Following this article, rope twist φ changes over its arc-length s as follows (equation (54.1)):

    ∂ φ ∂ s = ν 3 − τ

    where

    • φ is rope angle of twist with respect to its Frenet frame,
    • s is position along the rope's path,
    • ν 3 is rope torsion,
    • τ is the geometric torsion of the helix formed by rope's centreline.

    When the rope slides around the bar, we want to measure whether it rotates over time, expressed as ∂ φ ∂ t . A bent rope resists turning about its own axis, so as the rope slides, each piece of it preserves its angle φ to the bar. In a short time d t , a piece of rope moves along the bar by d s = v ⁢ d t , where v is the velocity of the rope, and its angle stays constant:

    d φ = ∂ φ ∂ s ⁢ d s + ∂ φ ∂ t ⁢ d t
    0 = ( ∂ φ ∂ s ⁢ v + ∂ φ ∂ t ) ⁢ d t

    Therefore:

    ∂ φ ∂ t = − v ⋅ ∂ φ ∂ s

    Substituting (1), we get the rotation rate:

    ∂ φ ∂ t = v ⋅ ( τ − ν 3 )

    For simplicity, we assume that the rope has no torsion ( ν 3 = 0 ). After labelling the rotation rate ω = ∂ φ ∂ t and expanding τ (equation (31) from the article):

    τ = sin ⁡ α ⋅ cos ⁡ α R + r

    we finally get the equation below.

    ω = v ⋅ sin ⁡ α ⋅ cos ⁡ α R + r

    where

    • ω is the rotation rate of the rope about its axis (in radians per second),
    • v is the rope's velocity,
    • α is the wrap angle,
    • R is the radius of the bar,
    • r is the radius of the rope.
    Example calculation

    Take a 10 mm rope running over a carabiner whose bar is 10 mm thick, at an angle of 45°, where the spin is greatest. The radii are half the diameters: r = 5  mm and R = 5  mm . We want to calculate how much rope needs to be pulled over the carabiner to make the rope spin once.

    Dividing equation (7) by the rope's velocity v gives how much the rope turns per length of rope pulled over the bar:

    ω v = sin ⁡ 45 ° ⋅ cos ⁡ 45 ° 5  mm + 5  mm = 0.5 10  mm = 0.05  rad/mm

    One full turn is 2 ⁢ π radians, so the length of rope pulled per turn is

    2 ⁢ π 0.05  rad/mm ≈ 126  mm ≈ 12.6  cm

    So the rope turns once about its own axis for every 12.6 cm pulled over the carabiner: about 8 turns per metre. In practice, much less twist builds up than this, if the rope can't spin freely, because rope strongly resists such a large torsion.

    This result shows that rope naturally spins when it travels along a helical path. Note that this is not caused by friction, but by rope trajectory and material properties. Even with zero friction, rope would still spin.

    Importantly, spin rate is independent of the number of turns around the bar. A quarter turn induces the same spin rate as several full turns. This makes the result applicable to the rope running over a carabiner. However, there are factors which may limit the spin rate in such cases - more on that later.

    Some additional notes on this result.

    • Spin rate is zero when the rope is wrapped around the bar at a right angle.
    • Spin rate is highest for a rope running at 45° to the bar.
    • Spin rate is higher for smaller bend radii,
    • Rope tension and velocity do not play a role.

    You can play with some of the parameters affecting spin rate in Figure 4.

    1.2 Complex configurations

    The simple helical model above is sufficient to approximate some simple real-world examples, like a rope running through a tube-style device, or over a single carabiner. More complex systems, like the Munter hitch or a two-point anchor, can be described by decomposing them into multiple helical sections. To find how such a system behaves as a whole, we need to understand how multiple helical sections along the rope path interact.

    As we saw, a helix imparts a spin rate ω to the rope. When multiple helical sections are placed along the rope's path and their spin rates are the same, then the whole system's spin rate is equal to the sections' spin rates.

    But what if the spin rates are different? Rope running through a system of obstacles can only spin at one rate, because otherwise, torsion between the obstacles would accumulate without limit. The solution is simple: torsion accumulates up to a point, because once it is high enough, it balances any differences in spin rates between the obstacles.

    Mathematical derivation

    Consider a two-helix configuration. Let τ a and τ b be the geometric torsions of the two helices, and ν 3 a , ν 3 b be rope torsions at those helices. Because rope spin ω and velocity v must be equal for both, in a steady-state configuration, from equation (5), we have:

    ω v = const. = τ a − ν 3 a = τ b − ν 3 b

    If we assume a free-spinning rope, then torsion before the first helix is zero. Because rope preserves its angle φ around bars, as was discussed before equation (2), torsion around the first helix is also zero, ν 3 a = 0 . Therefore,

    ν 3 b = τ b − τ a

    which is non-zero, whenever the spin rates of the two helices are different. Thus, the second helix accumulates enough rope torsion to counter-act its spin difference to the first helix.

    The same reasoning applies to any number of sections in a row.

    As a general rule, the first obstacle sets how fast the rope spins. Every other obstacle only stores torsion around and between the obstacles, without affecting the overall spin rate. This effect is illustrated in Figure 5. This rather surprising result has implications for climbing anchors and belay devices, which are explored in Section 2.

    1.3 Rope dynamics

    The idealized model above has one big limitation. It assumes that the rope never rotates relative to its bend (equation (2)), and everything that follows rests on that. Real climbing ropes resist both torsion and bent rotation (Figure 6), and the two resistances compete. Bent rotation is resisted by friction inside the rope, which, like static friction, holds completely up to a limit. While the torsion a bar has to hold back stays within that limit, the rope does not rotate relative to its bend at all, and the idealized model from Section 1.2 holds exactly. Only past the limit does the rope slip: it rotates relative to its bend, and the bar forces less spin than the idealized model predicts.

    In practice, both of these cases seem to be quite common.

    Mathematical derivation

    Let β be the bent rotation rate: how fast a piece of rope turns relative to its bend as it travels along its path. The idealized model assumes β = 0 , which gives equation (5). Two torques act on the rope about its own axis. The first is the twisting moment carried along the rope. It is elastic, and proportional to rope torsion:

    M 3 = C ⁢ ν 3

    where C is the rope's torsional stiffness.

    The second torque resists bent rotation. We model it as friction: a torque per unit length of rope, m b , independent of the rotation rate:

    { m b = − m 0 ⁢ sgn ⁡ β if  β ≠ 0 | m b | ≤ m 0 if  β = 0

    where m 0 is the largest torque the friction can exert. First row is dynamic friction, second row is static friction.

    Presumably, m 0 grows with rope bend κ , and with rope tension T :

    m 0 = m 0 ⁡ ( κ , T )

    Without precise measurements, we can only guess at the exact formula. Something like this is plausible:

    m 0 ≈ μ b ⁢ κ 2 + μ T ⁢ T ⁢ κ

    The first term is friction inside the bent rope: turning a bent rope about its own axis takes each fibre from the outside of the bend to the inside and back, and the strands rub against each other as they do. The second term comes from the load pressing the rope against the bar, T ⁢ κ per unit length. The load flattens the rope's cross-section, and axial rotation rolls the rope's material through the flattened shape, losing energy. It also tightens the sheath braid around the core, pressing the strands together harder. μ b and μ T are scaling factors.

    On a helix, the curvature κ is

    κ = cos 2 ⁡ α R + r

    Neglecting rotational inertia, the torques on every piece of rope balance. For a rope which bends equally easily in every direction, bending moments do not contribute to the balance about the rope's axis, which leaves

    ∂ ν 3 ∂ s = − m b C

    So torsion can change along the rope only where the rope is bent. On straight sections, κ = 0 and torsion is constant, or zero if the end of the rope is free to rotate.

    Around a helix, following a piece of rope,as in equation (2), and substituting (1), its bent rotation rate β is

    β = ∂ φ ∂ t + v ⁢ ∂ φ ∂ s = ∂ φ ∂ t + v ⁢ ( ν 3 − τ )

    In steady state, torsion does not change over time, so ∂ φ ∂ t = ω is the same everywhere along the rope, so:

    β = ω − v ⁢ ( τ − ν 3 )

    As per equation (11), this equation can be split into two regimes:

    1. Static regime, internal rope friction holds ( β = 0 ). No rotational slip occurs, and torsion ν 3 is constant as the rope travels along the helix.

    2. Slip regime, internal rope friction is overcome ( β ≠ 0 ). Here, friction exerts all of m 0 , so by (15), torsion changes at exactly the rate m 0 C , and by differentiating (17) and substituting:

    ∂ β ∂ s = v ⋅ ∂ ν 3 ∂ s = v ⋅ m 0 ⁢ sgn ⁡ β C

    Thus, | β | is increasing linearly in the direction of rope travel.

    Putting this all together, knowing that β is continuous, the overall picture is this. Let's say incoming torsion into the helical section is smaller than the outgoing torsion. Rope maintains its ingoing torsion as it travels over the helix at first, being in the static regime. At some point, the twisting moment overcomes rotational friction, rope starts rotating (slip regime), and torsion starts increasing linearly. As the rope leaves the helix, the torsion is equal to the outgoing torsion - much like a belt creeping over a pulley.

    In case the difference between torsions is very big, the rope may be in the slip regime along the whole path, which would lead to some of the torsion being relieved by "escaping" over the helix to the other side. This effectively limits the torsion difference a helix is able to hold to a maximum value, which is determined by the properties of the rope and the bar.

    It may be possible to measure the properties of m 0 ⁡ ( κ , T ) by observing how maximum torque behind a pulley depends on bend radius κ and rope tension T .

    From equation (18), we can calculate the maximum torsion difference a helix is able to hold, Δ ⁢ ν 3 , assuming for simplicity that tension T is constant:

    Δ ⁢ ν 3 = ∫ 0 L m 0 C ⁢ d s = m 0 ⁢ L C

    Substituting L from the equation for the wrap angle ψ :

    L = ψ ⁢ ( R + r ) cos ⁡ α
    Δ ⁢ ν 3 = ψ ⋅ m 0 C ⋅ R + r cos ⁡ α

    Substituting the m 0 guess from (13) and simplifying using (14):

    Δ ⁢ ν 3 ≈ ψ ⋅ cos ⁡ α ⋅ μ b ⁢ κ + μ T ⁢ T C

    If every bar has torsion difference within the Δ⁢ ν 3 , limit, the rope follows the idealized model from Section 1.2. Otherwise, the rope "slips", its twisting action is limited, and torsion difference is kept at the maximum value Δ⁢ ν 3 .

    1.4 Rope buckling

    The last factor influencing rope twist that I found is rope buckling. So far, we have assumed that the rope runs along a fixed trajectory, which runs straight between obstacles, and tightly wraps around obstacles. However, when enough torsion accumulates, the rope will bend in a way to relieve some of the accumulated torsion. This will, in turn, prevent the rope from twisting further.

    This effect is strongly dependent on rope tension. If tension is very low, the rope is free to find a shape which eliminates torsion accumulation. If tension is very high, the rope is forced to follow the straightest path, and if that path is helical, torsion will start accumulating.

    The practical upshot is that loosely handled ropes will twist much less than tightly held ropes. Belaying over a Munter hitch, for example, should be done by feeding the rope through the hitch, rather than pulling it by force.

    As shown above, the model predicts rope to twist quite severely, regardless of how many turns the rope has run around the bar.     2 Applications

    For readers who skipped Section 1, here is a summary of the main points, as they apply to the practical applications below:

    • Climbing ropes naturally spin when running around obstacles at an oblique angle. This spin is what causes ropes to twist and tangle.
    • The idealized case is rope running around a bar along a spiral path. Real rope paths can be decomposed into a series of such spiral segments.
    • Obstacles placed at an angle of 45° to the rope cause the maximum amount of rope twist.
    • The amount of twist depends on rope tension. With more tension, ropes will tend to spin more.
    • When the rope runs around multiple obstacles, the first obstacle (i.e., where the rope goes in) will determine the amount of spin, other obstacles don't have much effect.
    • Likewise, in belaying and rappelling, shape of the first bend will determine the amount of spin. Hand positioning is thus very important.

    Based on this understanding, we can now analyze how the rope gets twisted in practice, and see how it can be minimized or eliminated. These are the main ways in which ropes get twisted:

    • Lowering through anchors,
    • Belaying and rappelling,
    • Improper coiling and un-coiling.
    Each of these is discussed in detail in the following sections:

    2.1 Lowering through anchors

    Lowering through two rings placed side-by-side is known to sometimes twist ropes. The main problem with this setup is not the doubled anchor itself, but the fact that it sometimes forces the rope to run through the rings at an oblique angle. This is nearly impossible with one ring, or two rings which are touching each other, as the rope will align to be perpendicular to the ring(s) in this case.

    Experience shows that it's better to place the rings mirrored (Figure TODO), rather than facing the same side (Figure TODO). But as we discussed in Theory, neither of these configurations will completely eliminate rope twist. The two twists do not cancel each other out, even if they are opposite. Instead, the first ring along the rope path will apply its twist, and the second ring will have no influence, or only a limited influence.

    To eliminate rope twist, both rings should be aligned perpendicular to the rope (Figure TODO), or be free to move and align themselves (Figure TODO).

    2.2 Belaying and rappelling

    2.2.1 Tube-style devices

    Tube-style devices don't twist ropes, because all bends inside the device are in one plane, α = 0 in equation (6).

    2.2.2 Gri-Gri

    Gri-Gri can twist a rope, because the rope runs over the lip at an angle, but the effect is typically limited, and dependent on how the rope is handled. Normally, the break side of the rope is held very loose with Gri-Gri, but people used to tube-style devices may hold more tension, which will cause some rope twist.

    2.2.3 Munter hitch

    Munter hitch is notorious for twisting the rope, but it is in fact, avoidable. From theory, we know that the last bend causes most, or all, of the twist. So everything depends on how the break side of the rope is handled. If the rope is held in a way which constitutes a helix, the rope will twist. But if the last bend is planar, it will not.

    When belaying with a Munter hitch, we can limit the twist by handling the rope very loosely - feeding the rope into the knot before pulling it out. That way, the bends inside the knot have a large radius R , and the rope can move around to minimize the twist, as discussed in Section 1.4. We should also prevent a spiral from forming over the carabiner (Figure TODO), or better yet, place the breaking stand next to the outgoing strand - but that is generally not necessary.

    When lowering or rappelling, the tension in the rope is considerable, and it is not possible to handle the break strand loosely. Thus, the last bend in the Munter will twist the rope considerably. The way to prevent it is to hold the break side up, which creates a new zero-twist bend, but that is very awkward. A better alternative is to pass the break side of the rope over your head, and make an unfinished super-Munter hitch, which I find works very nicely. Full super munter can also be used, but it breaks too much.

    The super Munter does not twist the rope for the same reasons we saw before - the bend radius R is large, the break side of the rope is loose, and the angle α is small.

    2.2.4 Figure-8 belay device

    Figure-8 can be held in two ways. One way (Figure 9, A) contains two helices - one clockwise, and the other counter-clockwise. But, as we saw before, they don't cancel each other out: the first twist is the only one that matters. Thus, the proper way of holding the break side is down (Figure 9. B). This basically eliminates the incoming spiral, and replaces it with an α = 0 wrap which minimizes rope twist.

    2.3 Rope coiling

    Contrary to popular belief, coiling and un-coiling rope in a circular coil (also known as Alpine coil) does not twist the rope. Or, more precisely, coiling it correctly does not. If you coil the rope circularly, you need to ensure that each coil contains a single rope twist. That happens naturally when coiling, and it can be identified by the rope forming a figure-8-like loops, not a circular loops (Figure 10). If this is ensured, the rope does not need to be flaked, and it doesn't tangle, because every twist is undone when pulling the rope out of the coil.

    If you try to make the coil circular, like a coiled garden hose (Figure 10,A), you will see that you need to twist the rope as you go. If you then don't undo those twists in the same way, the rope will get tangled as it is pulled out. This is sometimes a problem with brand new ropes, which may be packaged in this kind of circular coil.

    Untwisting a rope

    I have not tested these extensively. But according to the theory, I can make the following guesses as to what will untwist a rope:
    1. Flake the rope, watch how it twists (i.e., watch its torsion), and manually undo it so that the sheath is straight again. This is the obvious option, and the one I used to untwist my ropes after all the experiments for the article. It works well.
    2. Hang the rope freely, whole or in segments. This should remove most torsion, because ropes resist torsion. People report that this works, but you need to have a cliff.
    3. Run the rope through a tube-style device, or a Gri-Gri. Keep the break side loose, so the rope can find the path minimizing torsion. People report that this works to an extent, but it may need to be repeated several times.
