    Why Ropes Twist

    Climbing ropes sometimes twist and tangle, but the precise mechanism of how and when is not well understood in the climbing community. Which belay devices, anchor configurations, or coiling methods cause the twist? Does it depend on friction, climber weight or lowering speed? What even is rope twist? This article explores the physics of rope twisting to answer these questions.

    Pavel Potoček · October 2026

    Theory

    Rope twist is not an invisible or mysterious property, nor is it caused by the rope sheath moving relative to the rope core (at least not in most cases). Rope twist is simply the rotation of a rope around its own axis. When one end of the rope is held stationary, and the other end is spun, the rope accumulates twist. When the number of twists is high enough, the rope starts buckling and eventually forms loops and becomes tangled.

    Because climbing rope sheath is diagonally braided, ropes bend easily, but are very resistant to torsion - a technical term for twisting along a rope's length. Given this property, when running around obstacles, ropes will bend, but will remain mostly untwisted. It turns out, rather surprisingly, that this tendency to remain untwisted when following curved paths is what makes the rope spin, and as a result, become twisted and tangled.

    In order to quantify this effect, we need to understand the details of how ropes behave when running around obstacles. We can model rope as a curve running through space. When the curve is planar, the rope bends in an obvious fashion, and no twist is induced. This is the case for a straight rope, but also rope running over a pulley, through a tube-style device, or through some simple anchor configurations.

    Mathematical derivation

    The mathematical model comes from Kirchhoff's rod theory. Following this article, rope twist φ changes over its arc-length s as follows (equation (54.1)):

    ∂ φ ∂ s = ν 3 − τ

    where

    • φ is rope angle of twist with respect to its Frenet frame,
    • s is position along the rope's path,
    • ν 3 is rope torsion,
    • τ is the geometric torsion of the helix formed by rope's centreline.

    When the rope slides around the bar, we want to measure whether it rotates over time, expressed as ∂ φ ∂ t . A bent rope resists turning about its own axis, so as the rope slides, each piece of it preserves its angle φ to the bar. In a short time d t , a piece of rope moves along the bar by d s = v ⁢ d t , where v is the velocity of the rope, and its angle stays constant:

    d φ = ∂ φ ∂ s ⁢ d s + ∂ φ ∂ t ⁢ d t
    0 = ( ∂ φ ∂ s ⁢ v + ∂ φ ∂ t ) ⁢ d t

    Therefore:

    ∂ φ ∂ t = − v ⋅ ∂ φ ∂ s

    Substituting (1), we get the rotation rate:

    ∂ φ ∂ t = v ⋅ ( τ − ν 3 )

    For simplicity, we assume that the rope has no torsion ( ν 3 = 0 ). After labelling the rotation rate ω = ∂ φ ∂ t and expanding τ (equation (31) from the article):

    τ = sin ⁡ α ⋅ cos ⁡ α R + r

    we finally get the equation below.

    ω = v ⋅ sin ⁡ α ⋅ cos ⁡ α R + r

    where

    • ω is the rotation rate of the rope about its axis (in radians per second),
    • v is the rope's velocity,
    • α is the wrap angle,
    • R is the radius of the bar,
    • r is the radius of the rope.

    This shows that rope naturally spins when it travels along a helical path. Note that this is not a result of friction, but a result of rope geometry. Even with zero friction, rope would still spin. Likewise, rope tension does not play a role in this result.

    Some additional notes on this result:

    • Spin rate is highest for a rope running at 45° to the bar. The spin rate is zero when the rope is wrapped around the bar at a right angle.
    • Spin rate is higher for smaller bend radii,

    Spin rate is independent of the number of turns around the bar. A quarter turn induces the same spin rate as several full turns. This makes the result applicable to the rope simply bending over a carabiner. However, there are factors which limit the spin rate in such cases - more on that later.

    Example calculation

    Take a 10 mm rope running over a carabiner whose bar is 10 mm thick, at an angle of 45°, where the spin is greatest. The radii are half the diameters: r=5 mm and R=5 mm. We want to calculate how much rope needs to be pulled over the carabiner to make the rope spin once.

    Dividing equation (7) by the rope's velocity v gives how much the rope turns per length of rope pulled over the bar:

    ω v = sin⁡45°⋅cos⁡45° 5 mm+5 mm = 0.5 10 mm = 0.05  rad/mm

    One full turn is 2⁢π radians, so the length of rope pulled per turn is

    2⁢π 0.05 rad/mm ≈ 126 mm ≈ 12.6  cm

    So the rope turns once about its own axis for every 12.6 cm pulled over the carabiner: about 8 turns per metre. In practice, much less twist builds up than this, if the rope can't spin freely, because rope strongly resists such a large torsion.

    So far, the rope arriving at the bar was untwisted. In general, the rotation φ of the rope about its own axis can change in two different ways, which are easy to confuse (Figure 5). Below, l is the length of rope pulled past a fixed place, and s is the position along the rope at one moment:

    Quantity Meaning Definition
    Spin rate how fast the rope turns about its own axis at a fixed place, per length of rope pulled past it ω = ∂ φ ∂ l
    Torsion twist stored in the rope, held by torque: how its rotation changes along its length at one moment θ = ∂ φ ∂ s
    Twist how much a piece of rope turns as it travels, per length travelled ω + θ

    A bar gripping the rope fixes the twist:

    ω + θ = τ

    with τ from the formula above. A rope arriving untwisted ( θ = 0 ) spins at the full τ , as in Figure 4; a rope that already carries torsion τ runs around the bar without spinning at all.

    Complex configurations

    The simple helical model above is sufficient to approximate some simple real-world examples, like a rope running through a tube-style device, or over a single carabiner. More complex systems, like the Munter hitch or a two-point anchor, can be described by decomposing them into multiple helical sections. To find how such a system behaves as a whole, we need to understand how helical sections placed one after another along the rope interact.

    As we saw, a helix imparts a spin rate ω to the rope. When multiple helical sections are placed along the rope's path and their spin rates are the same, then the whole system's spin rate is equal to the sections' spin rates.

    At first glance, each helical section seems to force its own spin rate on the rope - but that is clearly impossible. Rope running through a system of obstacles can only spin at one rate, because otherwise, over time, torsion between two obstacles would accumulate without limit. The following sections analyze how the elementary helical sections interact with each other to produce the final spin rate.

    The same reasoning applies to any number of sections in a row, and gives a simple rule:

    The first helical section the rope runs through sets how fast the rope spins. Every section after it only stores torsion in the rope leading into it.

    For each section after the first, the rope leading into it settles at a torsion equal to the difference between that section's spin rate and the first section's. The rope leaving the last section is free, so it spins at the first section's rate.

    Limitations

    As shown above, the model predicts rope to twist quite severly, regardless of how many turns the rope has run around the bar. Even a slight 30-degree bend over an edge should induce significant twist - but that is not what we observe. I found two explanations which limit how much rope twist is induced in practice:

    Rope buckling

    When rope tension is low, rope twist is limited, because the rope is not forced to follow the straightest path around the obstacle. Instead, it will change its shape in ways which limit the intrinsic rotation, and thus limit the induced twist.

    Rope slipping

    Normally, the rope follows the zero-strain path around the obstacle. But when the number of turns is low, or when the turn radius is large, the rope may deviate from the zero-strain path, and rotate relative to the bar to relieve some of the accumulated torsion. This, again, limits the induced twist.

    Applications

    Rope can become twisted in several ways:

    • Improper coiling and un-coiling,
    • Running the rope through some configurations of fixed points,
    • Belaying or rappelling with a device that causes the rope to twist.
